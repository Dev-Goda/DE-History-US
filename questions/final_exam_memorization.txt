CHAPTER: Recurring Themes

Q: "Formal vs. Substantive Equality" refers to:
1: The gap between what a law says on paper and what it actually produces in practice
2: The difference between state law and federal law in the Jim Crow era
3: The legal standard used in Brown v. Board of Education to overturn Plessy
4: A political theory developed by W.E.B. Du Bois to challenge Booker T. Washington
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: A law can be race-neutral on its face yet produce discriminatory outcomes. The 15th Amendment formally banned voter discrimination but poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses made it meaningless. The gap between legal promise and lived reality runs through every unit on the exam.

Q: Which recurring pattern does the study guide call "The Limits of Reform"?
1: Reform laws are always repealed within a generation by conservative backlash
2: Reform consistently reflects the boundaries of public sympathy rather than full public need, leaving whole constituencies behind
3: Reformers are always co-opted by business interests before achieving real change
4: Progressive legislation tends to expand the federal government beyond what the Constitution allows
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Jungle exposed labor exploitation but the public only cared about food safety. The Social Gospel called for justice but ignored Jim Crow. The GI Bill built a middle class but primarily for white male veterans. Pattern: every reform era has a constituency it ignores.

Q: The "Cold War Contradiction" refers to the fact that the U.S.:
1: Supported communist governments in Asia while fighting them in Europe
2: Championed democracy and self-determination abroad while denying them at home and backing colonial powers overseas
3: Refused to join the United Nations despite being its primary architect
4: Spent heavily on defense while cutting domestic programs that would have strengthened the economy
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The U.S. proclaimed democracy for all peoples while maintaining Jim Crow at home and backing France in Vietnam and Britain in Iran against independence movements. This contradiction damaged U.S. credibility throughout the Global South.

Q: The study guide identifies "Government Spending & Economic Growth" as a recurring theme. What does it argue?
1: Government spending always produces inflation and should be kept minimal
2: Federal spending drove economic growth — from the New Deal to WWII mobilization to postwar prosperity — rather than hindering it
3: Private investment, not government spending, was the engine of all major American growth eras
4: Tax cuts for corporations consistently produced more growth than direct government expenditure
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The failure of laissez-faire in the Depression and the success of wartime Keynesian deficit spending are two sides of the same argument. The government spending that built the interstate highway system, won WWII, and created the postwar middle class contradicts the idea that government is inherently a drag on growth.

Q: "Race, Rights & Structural Inequality" as a recurring theme means that:
1: American law has consistently failed to distinguish between racial groups
2: Legal equality rarely produced economic equality — each era's "solution" required the next generation to fight for the same promise
3: The civil rights movement was ultimately successful in eliminating both legal and economic racial inequality
4: Racial inequality in America is primarily a cultural rather than a structural problem
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Sharecropping trapped freed people in debt after slavery ended. Redlining locked Black Americans out of wealth-building after the GI Bill. Legal equality (13th, 14th, 15th Amendments; Civil Rights Acts) was never automatically accompanied by economic equality. Each generation inherited the incomplete work of the last.

CHAPTER: Unit 1 — Reconstruction & the Gilded Age (c.1865-1900)

Q: Sharecropping replaced chattel slavery but preserved economic dependence through:
1: Convict leasing and chain gangs organized by state governments
2: Debt peonage — a cycle of debt that prevented freedpeople from accumulating wealth or leaving
3: Federal land grants given exclusively to white landowners after the Civil War
4: Poll taxes that consumed most of a freedperson's annual income
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Under sharecropping, freedpeople rented land and paid with a share of their crop. Merchants extended credit at high interest rates for tools and food — keeping workers in perpetual debt. The system was designed to replicate slavery's labor control without the legal label.

Q: Manifest Destiny is best defined as:
1: The 19th-century belief that the U.S. was divinely ordained to expand westward across the continent
2: The policy of granting citizenship to all peoples absorbed by U.S. territorial expansion
3: An economic theory justifying railroad monopolies as a national necessity
4: The congressional doctrine that authorized the removal of Native peoples from the Southeast
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Manifest Destiny held that American expansion was God's plan. It drove railroad construction, displacement of Native peoples, and the Spanish-American War's imperial reach beyond the continent.

Q: The Gilded Age is characterized by all of the following EXCEPT:
1: Rapid industrialization and business consolidation into trusts and monopolies
2: Government subsidies for railroads
3: Massive consumption
4: Conservation of natural resources as a national priority
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: Conservation was NOT a Gilded Age value — it became a Progressive Era concern under Theodore Roosevelt. The Gilded Age was about extraction, consumption, and growth with minimal government restraint.

Q: The key distinction between the Gilded Age and the Progressive Era is that:
1: The Gilded Age produced economic growth while the Progressive Era produced economic stagnation
2: The Gilded Age treated government as an enabler of business; the Progressive Era treated government as a corrective to industrial excess
3: The Gilded Age saw mass immigration while the Progressive Era imposed immigration restriction
4: The Gilded Age was dominated by Democrats; the Progressive Era by Republicans
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Gilded Age: industrial growth, consumption, consolidation, laissez-faire. Progressive Era: reform, regulation, muckraking, settlement houses, trust-busting. The fundamental shift was in what government was FOR — passive enabler vs. active regulator.

Q: During the immigration waves of 1870-1890, southern and eastern Europeans and Asian immigrants:
1: Were welcomed into established labor unions as a source of cheap labor
2: Received government land grants in the West under the Homestead Act
3: Settled in ethnic enclaves due to discrimination; labor unions were often hostile to their arrival
4: Were rapidly assimilated into American culture through settlement house programs
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Discrimination pushed new arrivals into ethnic neighborhoods. Established craft unions (especially the AFL) often excluded immigrants and people of color, viewing them as wage-undercutting competition rather than potential allies.

Q: The "disenfranchisement machinery" used by Southern states after Reconstruction included:
1: The Black Codes, sharecropping contracts, and convict leasing
2: Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and all-white primaries
3: Redlining, restrictive covenants, and FHA discrimination
4: Separate-but-equal facilities validated by Plessy v. Ferguson
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: These tools were carefully designed to be facially race-neutral (technically compliant with the 15th Amendment) while targeting Black voters almost exclusively. Grandfather clauses exempted men whose grandfathers had voted — which excluded all formerly enslaved people.

Q: Booker T. Washington's strategy for Black advancement was:
1: Immediate demand for full political equality and voting rights
2: Economic accommodation within the existing racial order — build wealth and prove worth first
3: Legal challenges to Jim Crow laws through the federal court system
4: Political organizing through the NAACP to pressure Congress
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Washington's "Atlanta Compromise" (1895) urged Black Americans to accept social segregation temporarily and focus on vocational training and economic self-sufficiency. He believed demonstrated economic value would eventually earn political rights.

Q: W.E.B. Du Bois disagreed with Washington by demanding:
1: A return to Africa as the only path to genuine freedom
2: Immediate, full political equality — the vote, civil rights, and higher education
3: A gradual, legalistic strategy of challenging Jim Crow one law at a time
4: Federal reparations for slavery as a precondition for political participation
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Du Bois coined the concept of the "Talented Tenth" — the idea that higher education would produce Black leaders who could fight for civil rights. He co-founded the NAACP in 1909 and spent his life demanding immediate equality, not patient accommodation.

CHAPTER: Unit 2 — Progressive Era (c.1900-1920)

Q: Muckrakers were:
1: Progressive politicians who introduced reform legislation in Congress
2: Investigative journalists who exposed corruption, corporate abuse, and social injustice
3: Settlement house workers who documented poverty in urban immigrant neighborhoods
4: Union organizers who used illegal strikes to expose unsafe working conditions
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Key muckrakers: Upton Sinclair (The Jungle — meatpacking), Ida Tarbell (Standard Oil), Lincoln Steffens (city corruption). Their impact was often narrower than intended — The Jungle produced food safety law, not labor protections.

Q: Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle intending to expose labor exploitation, but it produced:
1: The Sherman Antitrust Act, breaking up the meatpacking monopolies
2: The Pure Food and Drug Act — the public cared about their food, not the workers
3: The Wagner Act protecting workers' right to organize
4: The Fair Labor Standards Act setting minimum wages and maximum hours
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Sinclair wanted to shock readers into demanding labor reform. Instead, public disgust focused entirely on the contaminated food. Sinclair later said, "I aimed at the public's heart and by accident hit it in the stomach." This is the "Limits of Reform" pattern.

Q: Ida Tarbell's investigative journalism targeted:
1: The meatpacking industry
2: Political machine corruption in American cities
3: John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil monopoly
4: Child labor in southern textile mills
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Tarbell's 19-part series in McClure's Magazine (1902-1904) documented how Standard Oil used secret railroad rebates, predatory pricing, and industrial espionage to crush competitors. It led directly to the Supreme Court breaking up Standard Oil in 1911.

Q: Lincoln Steffens' muckraking targeted:
1: Standard Oil's monopolistic practices
2: Political machine corruption in American cities — published as "The Shame of the Cities"
3: Child labor in Pennsylvania coal mines
4: Unsafe food and drug manufacturing
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Steffens documented how corrupt political machines (Tammany Hall in New York, bosses in St. Louis, Minneapolis, etc.) operated through bribery, patronage, and graft. His work built public demand for city government reform.

Q: Yellow journalism is associated with William Randolph Hearst and refers to:
1: Academic publications that oversimplified complex political issues for mass audiences
2: Sensationalist, partisan reporting that inflamed public opinion to sell papers
3: Government propaganda used to build public support for U.S. entry into WWI
4: The muckraker tradition of undercover journalism exposing corporate corruption
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Hearst's New York Journal published dramatic, often exaggerated stories about Spanish atrocities in Cuba. His coverage of the USS Maine explosion helped inflame public opinion and push the U.S. into the Spanish-American War (1898).

Q: The Triangle Shirtwaist Fire (1911) killed 146 workers primarily because:
1: Workers were unable to escape because the exits were locked — owners acquitted, illustrating courts protected capital over workers
2: Faulty boilers caused an explosion that collapsed the factory building
3: Strike-breaking violence by company guards trapped workers on upper floors
4: Fire escapes collapsed because the building was illegally constructed of wood
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Factory owners had locked the exit doors to prevent theft and unauthorized breaks. Workers on upper floors had no escape. The owners were acquitted. The fire made the case that courts protected capital over workers and created enormous political pressure for government factory regulation.

Q: The Social Gospel movement applied Christianity to:
1: Missionary work in Africa and Asia as part of U.S. imperial expansion
2: Temperance and Prohibition campaigns to reduce alcohol consumption
3: Social problems — poverty, urban slums, labor exploitation — calling for structural reform, not just individual salvation
4: The campaign for women's suffrage, framing the vote as a Christian moral duty
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Social Gospel advocates argued Christianity demanded addressing the causes of poverty. Its significant blind spot: largely silent on Jim Crow and women's rights, limiting its reform vision to white, male urban poverty.

Q: Jane Addams founded Hull House in Chicago as a:
1: Hospital providing free medical care to industrial workers injured on the job
2: Settlement house where middle-class reformers lived among the urban poor to provide services and document conditions
3: Labor union organizing center for immigrant workers in the meatpacking industry
4: Free school offering English literacy classes to new immigrants
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House in 1889, inspired by Jacob Riis's documentation of immigrant poverty. Settlement house workers provided childcare, English classes, and job training — and generated the research base for Progressive Era legislation.

Q: Jacob Riis is associated with:
1: Photographing and documenting immigrant poverty in New York City tenements — "How the Other Half Lives"
2: Writing The Jungle to expose meatpacking industry conditions
3: Founding the NAACP to fight racial discrimination in Northern cities
4: Organizing the first national labor strike against railroad companies
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Riis's How the Other Half Lives (1890) used photography to document overcrowded, unsanitary tenement conditions in New York. It shocked middle-class readers and directly inspired settlement house reformers including Jane Addams.

Q: Theodore Roosevelt's "New Nationalism" differed from Woodrow Wilson's "New Freedom" in that:
1: TR wanted to break up all large corporations, while Wilson accepted regulated big business
2: TR expanded federal power to regulate big corporations; Wilson wanted to break them up to restore competition
3: TR opposed antitrust enforcement entirely, while Wilson pursued aggressive trust-busting
4: TR focused on foreign policy reform, while Wilson concentrated on domestic economic regulation
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Roosevelt believed large corporations were inevitable and should be regulated by a strong federal government. Wilson believed monopolies should be broken up to restore free competition. In practice, both approaches expanded federal economic power, just with different philosophies.

CHAPTER: Unit 3 — World War I & the 1920s

Q: The Zimmermann Telegram (1917) was:
1: Germany's declaration of unrestricted submarine warfare, sinking American ships
2: A secret German offer to Mexico to join a military alliance in exchange for recovering Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona
3: A British intelligence warning to the U.S. about German plans to attack American ports
4: A peace proposal from Kaiser Wilhelm II to President Wilson before U.S. entry into WWI
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann sent a coded telegram to Mexico proposing an alliance. Britain intercepted and shared it with the U.S. The public outrage helped push Congress to declare war.

Q: The Great Migration (1910-1930) was primarily driven by:
1: The promise of the Homestead Act opening Western land to Black Americans
2: WWI's industrial labor demand in Northern cities as European immigration halted
3: Federal relocation programs that paid Black Southerners to move north
4: The violence of the 1919 Red Summer race riots that made Southern cities unsafe
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Over 1.6 million Black Southerners moved North between 1910 and 1930. The primary driver was WWI — Northern factories desperately needed workers as European immigration stopped and the draft pulled men into the military.

Q: By 1920, American urbanization had reached the milestone that:
1: More Americans worked in manufacturing than in agriculture for the first time
2: For the first time, more Americans lived in cities than on farms
3: New York City exceeded one million residents, becoming the first American megacity
4: The majority of American immigrants settled in cities rather than on rural homesteads
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The 1920 census showed the U.S. had crossed the urban threshold. This demographic shift fueled the Harlem Renaissance, jazz culture, Prohibition tensions, and a deep rural-urban cultural divide.

Q: Harding's 1920 campaign slogan "Return to Normalcy" promised:
1: Military rearmament to prevent future wars in Europe
2: A renewed push for civil rights after wartime racial tensions
3: Aggressive federal regulation of monopolies and trusts
4: Stability and a retreat from WWI disruption and Progressive Era activism
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: Harding's slogan appealed to Americans exhausted by WWI and the Progressive Era's moral crusades. "Normalcy" meant pro-business, limited government, and a step back from Wilsonian idealism — not a reform agenda.

Q: The Teapot Dome Scandal involved:
1: Vote-rigging in the 1920 election uncovered by the Justice Department
2: Secret bribes paid to Interior Secretary Albert Fall to lease federal oil reserves to private companies
3: Illegal sale of U.S. war bonds to foreign governments after WWI
4: Kickbacks to Harding's cabinet from railroad companies seeking land grants
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Interior Secretary Albert Fall secretly leased federal oil reserves at Teapot Dome, Wyoming to private oil companies in exchange for bribes. Fall became the first Cabinet member imprisoned for crimes committed in office. The scandal defined Harding's legacy.

Q: Calvin Coolidge's governing philosophy was best captured by the phrase:
1: "Speak softly and carry a big stick"
2: "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself"
3: "The chief business of the American people is business"
4: "Return to Normalcy"
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Coolidge's famous quote perfectly encapsulates his presidency: pro-business, low taxes, minimal regulation. He championed cutting the top income tax rate from 66% to 20%, believing a healthy business climate would lift all Americans.

Q: The 19th Amendment (1920) granted:
1: Women the right to vote
2: Prohibition of alcohol manufacture and sale
3: Direct election of U.S. Senators by popular vote
4: Equal civil rights protection regardless of race or gender
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: The 19th Amendment, the culmination of decades of suffragist activism, prohibited denying the vote based on sex. It took effect in 1920, in time for that year's presidential election.

Q: The 18th Amendment (1920) established:
1: Women's suffrage across all states
2: The graduated federal income tax
3: The direct election of U.S. Senators
4: Prohibition — banning the manufacture and sale of alcohol
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: The 18th Amendment was the product of the temperance movement. Ironically, it fueled organized crime as bootlegging became enormously profitable — one of the most significant unintended consequences in American legislative history.

Q: Alice Paul's major legislative goal after winning women's suffrage was:
1: A federal law mandating equal pay for equal work
2: A constitutional amendment guaranteeing women equal access to university education
3: A law prohibiting gender discrimination in federal hiring
4: The Equal Rights Amendment to guarantee full legal equality for women
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: After the 19th Amendment passed, Paul immediately pivoted to the ERA, arguing that suffrage alone was insufficient — women still faced systematic legal discrimination in employment, property rights, and family law.

Q: The Flapper represented the 1920s "New Woman" primarily through:
1: Organizing politically to extend suffrage to all disenfranchised groups
2: Demanding equal pay and working conditions in factories
3: Defying Victorian gender norms through fashion, music, dancing, and social behavior
4: Entering professional careers previously closed to women, such as law and medicine
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: The flapper — bobbed hair, short skirts, dancing to jazz — was a cultural statement, not primarily a political one. She rejected the constraints of 19th-century femininity (modesty, domesticity, chastity) in favor of pleasure, visibility, and self-expression.

Q: The Harlem Renaissance was:
1: A government program to provide arts funding to African American communities in New York
2: A real estate development that reshaped Harlem's demographics in the 1920s
3: An African American artistic, literary, and intellectual flowering centered in Harlem, NYC
4: A political movement to elect Black candidates to New York City office
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Writers like Langston Hughes and Zora Neale Hurston, musicians like Duke Ellington, and visual artists collectively created a new Black cultural identity that challenged racist stereotypes and asserted Black dignity and creativity on the national stage.

Q: The Scopes Trial (1925) involved a Tennessee teacher prosecuted for:
1: Distributing pacifist literature opposing U.S. entry into WWI
2: Allowing racially integrated seating in his classroom
3: Refusing to lead students in mandatory prayer
4: Teaching Darwinian evolution in violation of state law
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: John Scopes violated Tennessee's Butler Act, which prohibited teaching evolution in public schools. Clarence Darrow defended Scopes; William Jennings Bryan prosecuted. Though Scopes was convicted, the trial exposed Fundamentalism to national ridicule.

Q: The KKK revival of the 1920s differed from its Reconstruction-era predecessor because it:
1: Was primarily a Southern organization with no national political ambitions
2: Focused exclusively on opposing Black voting rights and political participation
3: Now targeted Catholics, Jews, and immigrants in addition to Black Americans, achieving membership in the millions
4: Operated primarily through legal lobbying rather than violence and intimidation
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: The 1920s KKK was a national phenomenon reaching millions of members, including significant presence in Northern states like Indiana. It expanded its targets to fit nativist anxieties of the era.

Q: Congress responded to 1920s nativist sentiment by:
1: Passing the Chinese Exclusion Act Extension banning all Asian immigration
2: Requiring all immigrants to pass English literacy and civic tests before admission
3: Creating a federal commission to evaluate and deport suspected radical immigrants
4: Enacting strict national-origins quota laws targeting Southern and Eastern Europeans
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: The Emergency Quota Act (1921) and Immigration Act (1924) established national-origins quotas that deliberately privileged Northern and Western European immigrants while sharply limiting Italians, Poles, Greeks, and other Southern/Eastern Europeans. Asian immigration was effectively banned entirely.

Q: "Buying on margin" in the 1920s stock market meant:
1: Purchasing stocks using borrowed money, paying only a fraction (as little as 10%) upfront
2: Trading stocks only when the market price was above the book value of the company
3: Investing exclusively in blue-chip industrial stocks rather than speculative ventures
4: Purchasing federal war bonds at a discounted price for guaranteed return
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Buying on margin meant investors could control $1,000 in stock with only $100 down. When prices dropped, brokers issued "margin calls" demanding repayment — forcing investors to sell, which drove prices down further. This feedback loop turned the October 1929 crash into a catastrophic collapse.

Q: Installment buying, which boomed in the 1920s, allowed consumers to:
1: Return goods to any participating store for store credit toward future purchases
2: Pool savings with neighbors to collectively buy expensive goods at wholesale prices
3: Receive government-subsidized loans to buy labor-saving home appliances
4: Purchase major goods on credit, paying in scheduled installments over time
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: By 1927, over 60% of cars were sold on credit. Installment buying extended to refrigerators, radios, and furniture — creating a consumer debt culture that looked prosperous but left households dangerously exposed when the Depression hit.

Q: Henry Ford's assembly line transformed American life primarily because:
1: It created unionized manufacturing jobs that raised working-class wages significantly
2: It proved that American manufacturing could produce goods faster than European competitors
3: It made the Model-T affordable for ordinary Americans, democratizing car ownership
4: It pioneered safety standards that dramatically reduced workplace injuries
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: By producing a Model-T every 10 seconds by 1925, Ford slashed costs until a factory worker could afford the product he built. Car registrations leaped from 9 million in 1920 to 27 million by 1929, reshaping American geography, culture, and commerce.

Q: The Jazz Singer (1927) was a landmark in film history because it:
1: Was the first film to feature an African American actor in a leading role
2: Introduced synchronized sound to motion pictures, ending the silent film era
3: Was the first American film to earn over $10 million at the box office
4: Was directed by the first woman to direct a Hollywood feature film
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Jazz Singer, starring Al Jolson, used the Vitaphone sound-on-disc system to synchronize dialogue and music with the film. It was the transition point between silent films and the "talkie" era.

Q: Charles Lindbergh's significance in the 1920s was his:
1: Setting the land speed record in a gasoline-powered automobile across the American West
2: First solo nonstop transatlantic flight from New York to Paris in 1927
3: First commercial passenger airline service connecting New York and Chicago
4: Designing the engine that made Ford's Model-T so efficient and affordable
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Lindbergh's 33.5-hour solo flight made him the decade's greatest hero. The "Spirit of St. Louis" flight electrified the world and symbolized the era's faith in technology, individualism, and American can-do spirit.

Q: Babe Ruth's cultural significance in the 1920s extended beyond baseball because:
1: He was the first professional athlete to publicly support civil rights for Black Americans
2: He became such a towering public figure — more famous than most politicians — that he embodied the decade's excess and optimism
3: He pioneered the use of endorsement deals that transformed how athletes earned income
4: He was the first athlete to have a nationally broadcast radio contract
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Ruth's superhuman home run records (including 60 in a single season in 1927) made him a larger-than-life figure. His 1927 salary of $80,000 was higher than President Coolidge's.

Q: Why was the 1920s economy structurally fragile? The study guide identifies four weaknesses:
1: Foreign debt, oil dependence, farm overproduction, and anti-union laws
2: Stock speculation on margin, consumer credit, overproduction, and bank fragility with no deposit insurance
3: Immigration restriction, high tariffs, railroad monopolies, and declining manufacturing
4: Government deficit spending, gold shortage, high taxes, and wage inflation
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: All four weaknesses fed the crash's severity: (1) margin buying created forced selling cascades; (2) installment debt made households vulnerable; (3) factories produced more than consumers could buy; (4) without deposit insurance, bank failures became panics that spread contagion across the banking system.

CHAPTER: Unit 4 — Great Depression & World War II

Q: Black Tuesday refers to:
1: The day President Hoover declared a national banking emergency in 1930
2: The day the Smoot-Hawley Tariff was signed, triggering global retaliation
3: October 29, 1929, when the stock market suffered its catastrophic crash
4: The day the Bonus Army was forcibly dispersed from Washington D.C.
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: On Black Tuesday, stock values collapsed in panic selling. However, only 2.5% of Americans owned stocks — the crash revealed deeper structural weaknesses that would cause the Depression rather than causing it alone.

Q: The Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930) worsened the Great Depression by:
1: Raising prices on domestic goods, making them unaffordable for unemployed Americans
2: Redirecting federal revenue from relief programs to pay down the national debt
3: Triggering retaliatory tariffs from other nations that collapsed global trade from $36 billion to $12 billion
4: Causing European nations to default on WWI loans, destabilizing American banks
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: When the U.S. raised tariffs to record levels, trading partners retaliated. Global trade collapsed by two-thirds. U.S. exports fell 78%. This destroyed American agricultural and manufacturing exports, deepening unemployment that was already rising from domestic causes.

Q: "Hoovervilles" were:
1: Government-organized emergency shelter camps for the worst-affected unemployed Americans
2: Makeshift shantytowns built by the unemployed — the bitterly satirical name reflected public blame of Hoover for the disaster
3: Rural communities of Dust Bowl migrants who formed self-governing cooperatives
4: Federal work camps where the unemployed were assigned in exchange for food and shelter
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: These shantytowns of scrap wood and corrugated metal appeared in parks and on vacant lots in every major city. The name said everything about where Americans placed the blame for the catastrophe.

Q: Unemployment during the Great Depression peaked at approximately:
1: 10 percent of the workforce
2: 25 percent of the workforce
3: 17 percent of the workforce
4: 40 percent of the workforce
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: At its peak in 1932-33, roughly one in four American workers was unemployed. Black unemployment was estimated as high as 50%. These figures don't count the millions more who were underemployed.

Q: The Dust Bowl was caused by:
1: A combination of severe drought and decades of agricultural mismanagement that stripped the plains of protective topsoil
2: Federal irrigation projects that accidentally depleted the Great Plains water table
3: Industrial pollution from oil refineries that destroyed fertile soil in Oklahoma and Texas
4: Overuse of new chemical fertilizers that made soil brittle and susceptible to erosion
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Years of deep plowing had destroyed the native grasses that held Great Plains topsoil in place. When drought struck (1932-1936), there was nothing to anchor the soil. Massive dust storms — "Black Blizzards" — buried farms and drove hundreds of thousands from their homes.

Q: The Bonus Army of 1932 consisted of:
1: Active-duty military troops Hoover deployed to enforce emergency banking regulations
2: WWI veterans who marched to Washington demanding early payment of bonuses promised for 1945
3: Unemployed factory workers who marched on Washington to demand a federal jobs program
4: Farmers from the Dust Bowl region who marched to demand agricultural price supports
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: About 20,000 veterans camped in Washington. When Congress refused their demands and Hoover ordered MacArthur to clear them, MacArthur exceeded his orders — using cavalry, bayonets, and tear gas against veterans on American soil. A political catastrophe for Hoover.

Q: Douglas MacArthur's role in the Bonus Army (1932) incident was:
1: Refusing Hoover's order to use force and resigning in protest
2: Commanding the military operation that forcibly dispersed the veterans, exceeding Hoover's orders in his aggressiveness
3: Testifying before Congress that military force had been necessary and proportionate
4: Negotiating a peaceful settlement that gave veterans partial bonus payment
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Hoover ordered MacArthur to clear specific encampments; MacArthur exceeded these orders, driving veterans entirely from Washington using cavalry, bayonets, and tear gas. The spectacle of an American general attacking WWI veterans on American soil cost Hoover enormously.

Q: Hoover's "associationalism" philosophy held that:
1: Federal, state, and local governments should share equal responsibility for economic relief
2: Voluntary cooperation between businesses and private charities — not direct government action — should address economic hardship
3: Labor unions and corporations should negotiate wages and prices without government interference
4: The president should convene associations of business leaders to coordinate economic recovery plans
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Hoover genuinely believed that direct federal relief would undermine American self-reliance and create dangerous dependency. His POUR (President's Organization for Unemployment Relief) relied on private charity — which was quickly overwhelmed by a Depression that affected one-quarter of all workers.

Q: The Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC), Hoover's main anti-Depression tool, was controversial because:
1: It bought failing farms, called controversial because it excluded Southern farmers
2: It lent government money to banks and corporations at the top while providing no direct help to ordinary unemployed Americans — critics called it a "millionaire's dole"
3: It was a wartime emergency agency Hoover reactivated, called controversial because it was unconstitutional under peacetime law
4: It bypassed Congress by drawing on executive emergency funds
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Critics like Fiorello LaGuardia called the RFC a "millionaire's dole" — the government was bailing out financial institutions at the top while refusing direct aid to the unemployed at the bottom. It perfectly encapsulated Hoover's ideological blind spot.

Q: The First New Deal (1933-1934) focused primarily on:
1: Long-term structural reforms like Social Security and union rights
2: Immediate relief and economic recovery through emergency programs
3: Reforming the Supreme Court to remove obstacles to federal regulation
4: Military preparedness as an economic stimulus and job-creation strategy
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The First New Deal was a rapid response to emergency: bank deposit insurance (FDIC), emergency employment (CCC, PWA), agricultural price supports (AAA), and industrial coordination (NRA). Relief and recovery dominated; structural reform came in the Second New Deal.

Q: The Works Progress Administration (WPA) was:
1: The federal agency that regulated banks and implemented deposit insurance
2: The largest New Deal jobs program, employing 8+ million Americans on public works and arts projects
3: The agricultural program that paid farmers to reduce production and stabilize crop prices
4: The agency that distributed direct cash relief payments to unemployed Americans
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The WPA (later renamed Work Projects Administration) employed people to build roads, bridges, parks, post offices, and murals. FDR believed in work relief over direct handouts — the WPA was his largest implementation of the principle that government should be employer of last resort.

Q: The CCC, WPA, and PWA all shared which common purpose?
1: They provided emergency loans to banks to prevent further failures
2: They provided direct cash relief payments to unemployed Americans
3: They paid farmers to reduce crop production to raise agricultural prices
4: They used government spending to create jobs and build public infrastructure
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: The CCC employed young men in conservation projects; the PWA funded large-scale construction (dams, bridges, schools); the WPA employed millions in both construction and arts. Together they demonstrated that the federal government could be an employer of last resort.

Q: The Social Security Act (1935) created:
1: Universal healthcare coverage for all Americans
2: A federal jobs guarantee ensuring employment for all willing workers
3: Retirement pensions for elderly workers and unemployment insurance for the jobless
4: A minimum wage and maximum hours standard for all American workers
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Social Security established two pillars: old-age insurance (funded by payroll taxes) and unemployment insurance. It was the most transformative piece of New Deal legislation — creating the permanent safety net that still exists today.

Q: The Wagner Act (1935) was a landmark labor law because it:
1: Established a federal minimum wage of $0.25 per hour for all industries
2: Guaranteed workers' right to organize unions and bargain collectively, backed by federal enforcement through the NLRB
3: Banned child labor in all industries engaged in interstate commerce
4: Required corporations with over 100 employees to share profits with workers
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Wagner Act created the NLRB to enforce workers' right to organize without employer interference. Union membership surged from about 3 million to over 10 million by 1941.

Q: FDR's "court-packing scheme" was his attempt to:
1: Appoint Democratic loyalists to replace retiring Republican judges on federal district courts
2: Add six new Supreme Court justices after the Court struck down key New Deal programs
3: Remove sitting justices who had ruled against the New Deal through impeachment proceedings
4: Set mandatory retirement ages for Supreme Court justices over 70
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: After the Supreme Court struck down the NRA and AAA, FDR proposed adding one new justice for every sitting justice over 70. Congress — including Democrats — rejected it as an attack on judicial independence.

Q: The phrase "the switch in time that saved nine" refers to:
1: The Supreme Court's shift to uphold New Deal legislation in 1937 after FDR threatened court-packing
2: Congress's last-minute rejection of Prohibition repeal that preserved the 18th Amendment for nine more years
3: FDR's decision to enter WWII after nine months of neutrality following Pearl Harbor
4: The nine Senate votes that switched to defeat the Smoot-Hawley Tariff before it passed
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: After FDR threatened to pack the Court, Justice Owen Roberts shifted his votes to uphold New Deal legislation. Whether the threat caused the shift is debated, but the timing — the Court changed direction just as Congress was debating FDR's plan — is what the phrase captures.

Q: Eleanor Roosevelt's historical significance goes beyond being First Lady because:
1: She ran for a New York Senate seat while FDR was president, winning in 1936
2: She served as the U.S. Ambassador to the United Nations after WWII
3: She was appointed to FDR's cabinet as Secretary of Labor, the first woman in a cabinet post
4: She was a visible and influential advocate for the poor, women, and African Americans in ways no previous First Lady had been — holding press conferences and traveling the country
ANSWER: 4
EXPLAIN: Eleanor held press conferences (for women reporters only, forcing outlets to hire female journalists), traveled the country visiting relief programs, and advocated for Black Americans when FDR was politically unwilling to. She represented a new, activist model of the First Lady role.

Q: Huey Long challenged FDR from which political direction, and with what proposal?
1: From the left, with his "Share Our Wealth" plan calling for massive redistribution through taxation
2: From the center, arguing FDR needed to move faster on banking reform
3: From the right, arguing the New Deal was socialism that would destroy American freedom
4: From the far left, calling for the outright nationalization of major corporations
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Long's "Share Our Wealth" proposed capping personal fortunes at $5 million and guaranteeing every family a minimum income of $2,500. It was enormously popular — he claimed 7 million members — and posed a genuine political threat to FDR until Long's assassination in 1935.

Q: Dorothea Lange's "Migrant Mother" photograph became historically significant because:
1: It was the first photograph published on the front page of a major national newspaper
2: It won the first Pulitzer Prize ever awarded for a photograph
3: It put a human face on Dust Bowl suffering and became the iconic image of the Depression
4: It led directly to Congress passing emergency relief legislation for California farmworkers
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Lange's photograph of Florence Owens Thompson — hollow-cheeked, nursing children, surrounded by poverty — became the Depression's defining image. It demonstrated the power of documentary photography to create political empathy and move public opinion.

Q: A bank run occurs when:
1: Customers simultaneously withdraw deposits in panic, causing even a solvent bank to run out of cash and fail
2: Bank executives attempt to transfer funds to offshore accounts before regulators arrive
3: Federal regulators move to seize control of a bank suspected of fraud
4: A bank makes risky investments that cause it to lose its depositors' money
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Bank runs are self-fulfilling panics: if everyone believes a bank is failing, everyone withdraws at once, and the bank fails even if it was previously sound. The FDIC (created in 1933) ended bank runs by guaranteeing deposits — if your money is safe regardless, there's no reason to panic-withdraw.

Q: The Great Depression only truly ended because of:
1: WWII's massive industrial mobilization, which created full employment through wartime production
2: The banking reforms of the First New Deal, which restored confidence in financial institutions
3: The Second New Deal programs of 1935-36, which finally restored consumer spending
4: The Social Security Act (1935), which put purchasing power directly into elderly Americans' hands
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: By 1940 unemployment was still around 15%. It was wartime spending — on ships, planes, tanks, uniforms — that created full employment. The U.S. became the "Arsenal of Democracy," running factories 24 hours a day and actually experiencing labor shortages by 1943.

Q: The "Double V Campaign" launched by the Pittsburgh Courier called for:
1: Victory in Europe AND Victory in the Pacific — ending both theaters of war simultaneously
2: Victory against fascism abroad AND victory against racial discrimination at home
3: Victory for veterans' benefits AND victory for women's equal pay after the war
4: Victory for the New Deal's domestic programs AND victory over isolationist senators
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Double V (1942) argued Black Americans should fight fascism overseas while fighting Jim Crow at home. Black wartime service created enormous moral standing: if Black soldiers could die for democracy, how could the U.S. deny them it?

Q: "Rosie the Riveter" symbolized:
1: Women's wartime entry into industrial jobs previously held by men — and their postwar removal from those jobs
2: The labor union movement's expansion to include women workers during the New Deal
3: Middle-class women's postwar embrace of domestic life as depicted in 1950s advertising
4: The Progressive Era settlement house workers who organized immigrant women factory workers
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Millions of women entered defense industry jobs during WWII. After the war, returning soldiers displaced them — women were pushed back into domestic roles. This wartime experience of economic independence, then forced return to domesticity, planted the seeds of second-wave feminism.

Q: Japanese Internment during WWII — Executive Order 9066 (1942) — authorized:
1: The registration of German and Italian Americans in coastal military zones
2: The forced relocation of approximately 120,000 Japanese Americans into internment camps, about two-thirds of whom were U.S. citizens
3: The deportation of non-citizen Japanese nationals to Japan for the duration of the war
4: The suspension of habeas corpus for all persons of Asian ancestry on the Pacific Coast
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The internment of Japanese Americans — justified by wartime hysteria and racial prejudice rather than actual evidence of disloyalty — is one of the most significant civil liberties violations in U.S. history. The 1944 Korematsu decision upheld it; the U.S. apologized and paid reparations in 1988.

Q: The analytical key to understanding Japanese internment — why it was driven by race, not security — is:
1: The FBI had no evidence of Japanese American espionage or sabotage before internment began
2: German Americans and Italian Americans — also citizens of enemy nations — faced no mass internment despite Germany and Italy being equally at war with the U.S.
3: Military commanders opposed internment but were overruled by civilian politicians seeking votes
4: The Supreme Court later declared internment unconstitutional in a unanimous ruling
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Over 110,000 Japanese Americans were incarcerated without individual hearings, including U.S. citizens. Germany and Italy were equally at war with the U.S. — yet German-Americans and Italian-Americans faced no equivalent action. The differential treatment reveals race, not threat level, as the determining factor.

Q: Korematsu v. United States (1944):
1: Established that the president must seek congressional authorization before interning American citizens
2: Upheld the constitutionality of Japanese American internment — a decision later widely considered one of the Court's greatest failures
3: Required the federal government to pay compensation to Japanese Americans for property lost during internment
4: Struck down Japanese American internment as a violation of the 14th Amendment's equal protection clause
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The 6-3 decision upheld Executive Order 9066 on national security grounds. The dissents by Justices Murphy, Jackson, and Roberts were prophetic — Murphy called it "legalization of racism." The decision was formally overturned in 2018.

Q: The GI Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act, 1944) is critiqued because:
1: It provided insufficient benefits — veterans' payments were too low to actually purchase homes or attend college
2: Segregated universities rejected Black veterans, FHA redlining blocked Black homeownership, and women and gay veterans received fewer benefits — building a white middle class
3: It was funded by a tax that disproportionately burdened working-class Americans who hadn't served
4: Republicans blocked implementation until the Korean War, delaying benefits by nearly a decade
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The GI Bill's celebrated creation of the American middle class was primarily a white middle class. Black veterans were turned away from segregated universities. Federal Housing Administration guidelines (redlining) steered Black veterans away from suburban loans. The bill amplified, not reduced, existing racial wealth gaps.

Q: The Lend-Lease Act allowed the U.S. to:
1: Draft American men into military service before the U.S. officially entered WWII
2: Impose economic sanctions on Axis nations while maintaining formal neutrality
3: Supply Britain and later the Soviet Union with war materials before the U.S. officially entered the war
4: Seize Axis-owned assets and businesses within the United States
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Rather than sell weapons to Britain (which couldn't afford them), FDR proposed "lending" them, comparing it to lending a garden hose to a neighbor whose house is on fire. Lend-Lease eventually supplied $50 billion in aid to 30+ countries, making the U.S. the "Arsenal of Democracy" even before Pearl Harbor.

Q: Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941) is historically decisive because:
1: The destruction of the U.S. Pacific Fleet meant Japan now controlled all of the Pacific Ocean
2: The surprise attack killed 2,400 Americans and immediately brought the U.S. into WWII
3: FDR used the attack to justify declaring war on both Japan and Germany simultaneously
4: It was the first time in history a naval attack had been launched from aircraft carriers
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Pearl Harbor killed 2,400 Americans, wounded 1,200 more, and destroyed or damaged much of the Pacific Fleet. FDR called December 7 "a date which will live in infamy." Congress declared war on Japan the next day. Germany and Italy declared war on the U.S. four days later.

Q: The Battle of Midway (June 1942) was the turning point of the Pacific War because:
1: American forces landed on Japanese soil for the first time, demonstrating U.S. offensive capability
2: U.S. submarines destroyed Japan's oil supply fleet, crippling its ability to fuel future operations
3: The U.S. broke Japanese naval codes and used the intelligence to destroy four Japanese aircraft carriers, eliminating Japan's carrier superiority
4: Japan's failed landing attempt proved it lacked the amphibious capability to threaten Hawaii or the U.S. mainland
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: By cracking Japanese naval codes, American commanders knew Japan's plans and ambushed the attacking fleet. Losing four carriers meant Japan could no longer project power across the Pacific. From Midway onward, Japan was strategically on the defensive.

Q: D-Day / Operation Overlord (June 6, 1944) involved:
1: Troops from every Allied nation attacking simultaneously across five beaches
2: American forces crossing the Atlantic for the first time in WWII
3: Over 150,000 Allied troops crossing the English Channel to land at beaches in Normandy, France under Eisenhower's command
4: The liberation of Paris, which fell on the same day as the landings
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Operation Overlord involved 156,000 troops on the first day alone, supported by 11,000 aircraft and nearly 7,000 naval vessels. Eisenhower commanded. The Normandy breakout led to the liberation of Paris in August 1944 and eventually to Germany's defeat.

Q: The Manhattan Project was:
1: The top-secret U.S. program to develop the atomic bomb, directed scientifically by J. Robert Oppenheimer
2: The Allied deception operation that misled Germany about the location of the D-Day landings
3: FDR's program to rebuild New York City infrastructure during the Depression
4: A military industrial complex program to mass-produce tanks and artillery in New York State
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: The Manhattan Project employed over 130,000 people at sites across the U.S. and Canada. Oppenheimer led the scientific effort at Los Alamos, New Mexico. The first test (Trinity, July 1945) succeeded. Truman authorized the bomb's use on Japan in August 1945.

Q: The Tuskegee Airmen's historical significance was that:
1: They were the first Black soldiers allowed to serve in combat during WWII
2: They were Black officers who commanded integrated units in the European theater
3: They were African American pilots who served with such distinction that they helped dismantle arguments for segregating the military
4: They were the first Black military unit to serve on the front lines in the Pacific theater
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: The 332nd Fighter Group compiled an extraordinary combat record — escorting bombers with a remarkably low loss rate — directly refuting racist arguments that Black men couldn't be trusted with complex military technology or combat leadership. Their record contributed to Truman's 1948 desegregation of the military.

Q: The Holocaust refers to:
1: The Allied strategic bombing campaign that destroyed German industrial centers and killed civilians
2: The Nazi regime's systematic genocide of approximately 6 million Jews and millions of others, including Roma, disabled people, and political opponents
3: The wartime firebombing of Tokyo that killed more civilians than either atomic bombing
4: Japan's use of biological and chemical weapons against Chinese civilian populations throughout WWII
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Holocaust was not a byproduct of war but a deliberate, industrialized genocide. The Nazis built an infrastructure of deportation, forced labor, and extermination camps. Six million Jews — two-thirds of European Jewry — were murdered, along with millions of others deemed undesirable by Nazi ideology.

Q: The Zoot Suit Riots (1943) revealed what about wartime America?
1: The extent of labor union radicalism in wartime defense industries
2: That Japanese American internment had created dangerous resentment in other minority communities
3: Deep racial tensions on the American homefront, as white servicemen attacked Latino and Black youth in Los Angeles
4: That the federal government's wartime censorship had failed to suppress racial propaganda
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: White sailors and soldiers attacked young Latino and Black men wearing "zoot suits." Police arrested the victims, not the attackers. The riots exposed that the war for democracy abroad coexisted with virulent racism at home.

Q: V-E Day (May 8, 1945) marks:
1: The day Japan surrendered, ending all fighting in WWII
2: Germany's formal surrender, ending the war in Europe
3: The successful Allied landings at Normandy that opened the Western Front
4: The day the U.S. dropped the atomic bomb on Hiroshima
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: V-E Day (Victory in Europe Day) came after Hitler's death by suicide in his Berlin bunker on April 30, 1945. Germany signed an unconditional surrender on May 7; V-E Day celebrations erupted across Allied nations on May 8.

Q: Harry Truman's most consequential decision as president was:
1: Authorizing the use of atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, ending WWII and shaping the postwar nuclear world
2: Commanding American forces in the final Pacific island campaign
3: Negotiating the final terms of Germany's surrender at Potsdam
4: Creating the Marshall Plan to rebuild postwar European economies
ANSWER: 1
EXPLAIN: Truman authorized the bombings after being briefed on the Manhattan Project only upon becoming president. The decision remains one of history's most debated — defenders say it saved lives by avoiding an invasion of Japan; critics say it constituted a war crime against civilians.

CHAPTER: Unit 5 — Cold War, Civil Rights & the 1960s

Q: The Truman Doctrine (1947) pledged:
1: U.S. economic aid to rebuild war-devastated European economies to prevent communist recruitment
2: U.S. support for any nation threatened by communist subversion — initially Greece and Turkey
3: A massive U.S. military buildup to create overwhelming force to back containment
4: U.S. refusal to recognize any communist government established by force after WWII
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Facing communist insurgencies in Greece and a Soviet push on Turkey, Truman asked Congress to support "free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation." The shared assumption: poverty and instability breed communism; the U.S. must close those openings.

Q: The Marshall Plan (1948) was:
1: The U.S. military alliance with Western European nations against Soviet expansion
2: A $13 billion program to rebuild Western European economies devastated by WWII
3: The U.S. strategic doctrine committing to defending Europe with nuclear weapons
4: A foreign intelligence operation to monitor Soviet military movements in Eastern Europe
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Secretary of State George Marshall proposed massive U.S. economic aid to prevent the economic desperation that communist parties were exploiting across Western Europe. The shared assumption with the Truman Doctrine: poverty is communism's recruiter; prosperity is the shield.

Q: NSC-68 (1950) called for:
1: The creation of NATO as a permanent U.S. military commitment to Western European defense
2: A massive U.S. military buildup — quadrupling the defense budget — to back containment with overwhelming force
3: The use of nuclear weapons as a first-strike option against Soviet military concentrations
4: U.S. military intervention in Korea to repel the North Korean invasion of South Korea
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: NSC-68 argued the Soviet threat was existential and required the U.S. to spend whatever it took to maintain military superiority. It built on Kennan's containment logic but added: patiently resisting Soviet expansion requires credible military force to back it up.

Q: George Kennan's "containment doctrine" (the "X Article," 1947) argued that:
1: The U.S. should roll back Soviet power wherever possible through military force
2: The U.S. should negotiate a formal peace treaty with the Soviet Union
3: The U.S. should patiently resist Soviet expansion and wait for the USSR to collapse from internal contradictions
4: Nuclear deterrence alone was sufficient to prevent Soviet aggression
ANSWER: 3
EXPLAIN: Kennan argued the U.S. should not fight the Soviets directly but systematically resist their expansion. He believed Soviet communism would eventually collapse from its internal contradictions. His concept became the intellectual foundation for Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, and NSC-68.

Q: McCarthyism's lasting historical significance was:
1: The exposure and imprisonment of hundreds of actual Soviet spies in U.S. government agencies
2: A narrowed range of acceptable political discourse — anticommunist labeling suppressed labor organizing, civil rights advocacy, and social reform throughout the 1950s
3: The Army-McCarthy hearings' destruction of the communist party as a political force in America
4: The expansion of HUAC's power to investigate foreign governments suspected of espionage
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: McCarthy's personal downfall (Army-McCarthy hearings, 1954) did not end the Red Scare's effects. The tactic of labeling reform movements as communist successfully chilled labor organizing, civil rights work, and any advocacy for expanded government programs throughout the 1950s.

Q: HUAC's significance (Hoover testimony, 1947) was that it established the logic that:
1: Only card-carrying Communist Party members could be prosecuted for espionage
2: "Fellow travelers" — sympathizers without formal membership — were more dangerous and justified surveillance of virtually any dissent
3: Foreign governments, not domestic activists, were the primary threat to U.S. national security
4: Labor unions should be banned as instruments of Soviet economic warfare
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: FBI Director Hoover testified that "fellow travelers" — sympathizers, not members — were the real danger. This logic justified surveilling civil rights leaders, labor organizers, academics, and anyone who called for social reform. It equated domestic dissent with Soviet subversion.

Q: Brown v. Board of Education (1954):
1: Declared the poll tax unconstitutional under the 14th Amendment
2: Overturned Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine, extending the 14th Amendment to public education
3: Required the immediate and complete integration of all public schools within one year
4: First applied the equal protection clause to voting rights, laying the groundwork for the VRA
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Brown overturned Plessy's 1896 "separate but equal" ruling by finding that separate facilities were inherently unequal in education. Chief Justice Warren's unanimous opinion grounded it in psychological harm to Black children. However, "all deliberate speed" allowed massive Southern resistance.

Q: The Emmett Till case (1955) was historically significant because:
1: It was the first successful federal prosecution of a racially motivated murder in the South
2: His murderers were acquitted despite confessing publicly; Mamie Till's open casket forced the nation to confront racial violence — directly inspiring Rosa Parks and SNCC founders
3: His death in a Southern prison provoked the Montgomery Bus Boycott as a direct response
4: It led directly to the passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1957
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Emmett Till, 14, was murdered in Mississippi in 1955. His murderers were acquitted by an all-white jury in 67 minutes — then confessed publicly in Look magazine knowing they couldn't be retried. Mamie Till's open casket galvanized the civil rights movement.

Q: The sit-ins of 1960 were historically significant because:
1: They were organized by established civil rights organizations like the NAACP and SCLC
2: They were organized by students independently of established organizations, demonstrating young people's power to force desegregation — and led to the formation of SNCC
3: They were the first nonviolent direct action protest of the civil rights movement
4: They immediately produced federal legislation requiring desegregation of all public accommodations
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Greensboro sit-ins (February 1960) were organized by four students without organizational approval. Within months, sit-ins spread across the South. The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) emerged from this energy.

Q: Bloody Sunday / Selma (1965) was significant because:
1: The Supreme Court's reaction to the violence forced it to uphold the constitutionality of the Civil Rights Act
2: Televised images of police beating peaceful marchers gave LBJ the political momentum to push the Voting Rights Act through Congress
3: It marked the first time the National Guard was deployed to protect civil rights marchers in the Deep South
4: The violence forced President Kennedy to introduce civil rights legislation for the first time
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: On March 7, 1965, Alabama state troopers beat marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge on national television. LBJ went to Congress eight days later with his "We Shall Overcome" speech. The Voting Rights Act passed within five months.

Q: The Civil Rights Act of 1968 (Fair Housing Act) addressed:
1: Voting discrimination that had persisted despite the 1965 Voting Rights Act
2: Housing discrimination — the residential segregation that survived earlier civil rights laws
3: Employment discrimination in federal government agencies and contractors
4: School desegregation that had stalled under the "all deliberate speed" standard
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The 1964 Civil Rights Act covered public accommodations and employment. The 1965 VRA covered voting. The 1968 Fair Housing Act finally addressed residential segregation — the mechanism that kept Black Americans locked out of wealth-building through homeownership.

Q: The Voting Rights Act (1965) differed from the 15th Amendment (1870) in that the VRA:
1: Explicitly named race as a protected category, while the 15th Amendment used race-neutral language
2: Provided federal enforcement mechanisms — including federal registrars and preclearance requirements — that the 15th Amendment lacked
3: Applied to all elections nationally, while the 15th Amendment only covered federal elections
4: Set criminal penalties for voter suppression for the first time in American history
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The 15th Amendment formally banned voter discrimination in 1870 but provided no enforcement mechanism. Southern states spent the next 95 years circumventing it. The VRA created federal oversight and required Southern states to get Justice Department approval before changing voting rules — the teeth the 15th Amendment never had.

Q: Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963):
1: Documented the wage gap between men and women in American industry
2: Named "the problem that has no name" — educated middle-class women's dissatisfaction with domestic confinement — and launched second-wave feminism
3: Called for women's full inclusion in the civil rights movement
4: Argued that women should use the same nonviolent direct action tactics as the civil rights movement to demand the ERA
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Friedan interviewed her Smith College classmates 15 years after graduation and found educated women deeply unhappy despite having "everything." She named the collective unhappiness with domestic life "the problem that has no name." The book sold over a million copies and became the founding document of second-wave feminism.

Q: Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962):
1: Exposed the contamination of American drinking water by industrial chemical dumping
2: Documented DDT's environmental damage — killing bird populations — and launched the modern environmental movement, leading to the EPA and DDT ban
3: Called for federal regulation of nuclear testing and radiation exposure near American cities
4: Documented the destruction of old-growth forests by the logging industry
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Carson's book showed how DDT moved up the food chain, thinning bird eggshells and decimating raptor populations. It transformed how Americans thought about ecosystems. The book led directly to the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency (1970) and the federal ban on DDT (1972).

Q: The Great Society programs under LBJ included:
1: Social Security, the GI Bill, the Tennessee Valley Authority, and the Civilian Conservation Corps
2: Medicare, Medicaid, the Voting Rights Act, Head Start, and federal education funding
3: The Marshall Plan, NATO, the Truman Doctrine, and the National Security Act
4: The Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Equal Rights Amendment, Title IX, and the ERA
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: The Great Society was the most ambitious domestic program since the New Deal: Medicare (health insurance for the elderly), Medicaid (for the poor), the Voting Rights Act, Head Start (early childhood education), and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. Note: the ERA failed ratification in the 1970s.

Q: The Cold War Contradiction in decolonization: the U.S. stated value was self-determination, but U.S. actual policy was:
1: Supporting independence movements in Asia and Africa to weaken European colonial powers
2: Backing France in Vietnam, Britain in Iran, and other colonial powers when independence movements showed leftist leanings
3: Remaining neutral in all colonial conflicts to preserve relationships with both sides
4: Using the CIA to support only non-communist independence movements in the developing world
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Fear that independence movements would turn leftist led the U.S. to back colonial powers — but this identified the U.S. with colonialism rather than self-determination, pushing nationalist movements toward the Soviet Union and destroying U.S. credibility in the Global South.

Q: Television's evolution from the 1950s to the 1960s shifted from:
1: Government-controlled broadcasting to advertiser-supported commercial programming
2: Pursuing mass audiences (lowest common denominator) to targeted identity marketing — rebellion, sex appeal, individuality — in the "Creative Revolution"
3: Live programming to pre-recorded shows, allowing networks to reach rural markets
4: Regional programming to national programming funded by a small number of major sponsors
ANSWER: 2
EXPLAIN: Early TV aimed for the widest possible audience — safe, inoffensive content. By the 1960s, advertisers discovered they could target specific demographic groups more profitably. The "Creative Revolution" used irony, rebellion, and sex appeal to sell products to specific identity groups.
